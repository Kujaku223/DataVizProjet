The Recipe for Happiness: What Should We Really Ask of Our Governments?

Happiness can seem impossible to measure. After all, it depends on our relationships, our expectations, our health, our fears, our freedom, and our vision of the future. Yet when we look at data on an international scale, patterns begin to emerge.

Some countries, year after year, offer conditions in which citizens evaluate their lives more positively. Others remain associated with lower levels of perceived well-being. Happiness then becomes more than an individual emotion: it becomes an indicator of how a society allows its citizens to live, seek care, work, participate in collective life, and envision their future.

This website therefore explores a central question: what explains happiness at the population level, and what can Canada learn from it?

A Country’s Happiness Is a Trajectory, Not a Snapshot

The evolution chart shows that countries’ happiness scores do not change completely at random from one year to the next. The happiest countries often remain near the top of the ranking, while the least happy countries generally remain lower.

This suggests an important idea: collective happiness is not simply a matter of national mood or luck. It appears to be linked to deeper living conditions, which tend to change slowly over time.

The Ingredients of Collective Happiness

The happiness score from the World Happiness Report is based on how citizens evaluate their own lives. It is therefore a subjective measure, but this perception can be linked to several explanatory factors: GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, perceptions of corruption, and a residual component.

The stacked bar chart breaks down the happiness score of Canada over the years, since 2019. It shows that economic wealth matters, but it does not act alone. Social support, health, and freedom also play an important role in perceived well-being.

A happier population is not only a richer population. It is also a population that feels supported, healthy, free, and able to trust its social environment.

Beyond GDP

By isolating each factor, we can more clearly see what contributes to happiness. GDP per capita remains an important factor because it directly influences material living conditions. However, the data also shows the importance of social support and healthy life expectancy.

We also confronted the explanation factors to quantitative measures such as Human Development Index (HDI) and life expectancy in 2023 for comparison purposes. The HDI takes into account factors like life expectancy at birth, knowledge, and GINI index.

For Canadian voters, this observation is essential. Political debates often focus on economic growth, taxes, or productivity. These elements are important, but they are not enough on their own to explain a population’s well-being.

The question therefore becomes broader: what policies truly allow citizens to live better?

What Distinguishes the Happiest Countries from the Least Happy Ones

The charts comparing the happiest and least happy countries show that the top-ranking countries do not perform well on only one isolated factor. They generally present a more balanced profile: higher standard of living, strong social support, better health, greater freedom, and stronger social trust.

In contrast, the least happy countries often face multiple vulnerabilities at once. Their lower score does not seem to come from a single problem, but rather from an accumulation of economic, social, health-related, and institutional fragilities.

The Human Factor: Having Someone to Count On

Among the factors observed, social support is particularly meaningful. Behind this variable lies a simple question: if you had a problem, would you have someone to count on?

This question turns data into a societal issue. It points to loneliness, mental health, caregivers, social services, local communities, families, older adults, and newcomers.

For Canadian voters, the message is clear: improving collective happiness does not only mean improving economic indicators. It also means strengthening the social safety net and creating environments where people do not feel left alone when facing difficulties.

Canada: Well Ranked, but Which Pillars Must Be Protected?

Canada generally ranks among countries with a high level of perceived well-being. However, a good ranking does not mean there is nothing to improve. The cost of living, access to healthcare, mental health, housing, inequalities, social isolation, and trust in institutions can all influence the future trajectory of happiness in the country.

The goal of this website is therefore not simply to ask: is Canada happy?

The real question is rather: which factors must we protect or improve so that Canadians can live better? ?

This is where the Human Development Index becomes useful. By adding more objective indicators such as life expectancy, education, and income, we can compare perceived well-being with measurable development conditions. This allows us to see whether Canada truly transforms its development into quality of life as perceived by its population.

A country can be developed without maximizing the perceived well-being of its population. It is the gap between the two that deserves citizens’ attention.

Conclusion: Choosing a Well-Being Society

This analysis aligns with the WHO’s idea of “well-being societies”: societies that do not measure their success only through economic growth, but also through health, equity, social cohesion, quality of life, and sustainability.

For Canadian voters, happiness data does not provide a simple political answer. Instead, it offers a better lens for interpretation. It invites us to ask decision-makers more complete questions:

Do the proposed policies only improve economic indicators?

Or do they also improve health, social support, freedom, trust, and the concrete living conditions of citizens?

A country’s happiness is not only a collective emotion. It is the reflection of its societal choices.
